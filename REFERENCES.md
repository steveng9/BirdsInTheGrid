## Road ecology: how roads kill, and what reduces it

**Hels, T. and Buchwald, E. (2001).** The effect of road kills on amphibian populations. *Biological Conservation* 99(3): 331-340. doi:10.1016/S0006-3207(00)00215-9. [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0mf9859v)
Used for the traffic kill model: the probability of being killed crossing one lane is 1 - exp(-q a / v), with q vehicles per second, a the kill width, and v the animal's speed. Their estimates of the chance of being killed crossing were 0.34-0.61 on a road with 3,207 vehicles a day and 0.89-0.98 on a motorway. Abstract checked: it confirms that risk was calculated from traffic load and movement rate, but the exact equation has not been seen (the full text was unreachable on 2026-10-18), so the post cites Hels and Buchwald for those ingredients, not for the formula. The full text is also needed before the traffic parameters stop being placeholders.

**Rytwinski, T., Soanes, K., Jaeger, J. A. G., Fahrig, L., Findlay, C. S., Houlahan, J., van der Ree, R. and van der Grift, E. A. (2016).** How effective is road mitigation at reducing road-kill? A meta-analysis. *PLoS ONE* 11(11): e0166941. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0166941. [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5117745/)
A meta-analysis of 50 studies. Mitigation reduced road-kill by 40% on average; fences, with or without crossing structures, by 54%; crossing structures without fences showed no detectable effect. For large mammals, fencing plus crossing structures reduced road-kill by 83%. This is the field result the model reproduces in E3 and E5 (tunnels alone 43%, tunnels with a drift fence 99%). Abstract checked.

**Aresco, M. J. (2005).** Mitigation measures to reduce highway mortality of turtles and other herpetofauna at a north Florida lake. *Journal of Wildlife Management* 69(2): 549-560. [BioOne](https://www.bioone.org/journals/journal-of-wildlife-management/volume-69/issue-2/0022-541X(2005)069[0549:MMTRHM]2.0.CO;2/MITIGATION-MEASURES-TO-REDUCE-HIGHWAY-MORTALITY-OF-TURTLES-AND-OTHER/10.2193/0022-541X(2005)069[0549:MMTRHM]2.0.CO;2.full), [Conservation Evidence summary](https://conservationevidencejournal.com/individual-study/4783)
Temporary drift fences along a four-lane highway at Lake Jackson, Florida, steered turtles to an existing drainage culvert. Turtle road mortality fell from 11.9 to 0.09 per km per day, and 84 of 8,475 turtles got over or through the fence. The closest real case to the model's "underpasses + drift fence". Abstract and summary checked.

**Dodd, C. K., Barichivich, W. J. and Smith, L. L. (2004).** Effectiveness of a barrier wall and culverts in reducing wildlife mortality on a heavily traveled highway in Florida. *Biological Conservation* 118(5): 619-631. doi:10.1016/j.biocon.2003.10.011. (The USGS record titles it "...a barrier wall and underpasses..."; Crossref and the journal use "culverts".) [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1008513), [Conservation Evidence summary](https://conservationevidencejournal.com/individual-study/191)
A barrier wall with eight culverts on US 441 across Paynes Prairie. Road kills fell from 2,411 in the year before construction to 158 in the year after (excluding treefrogs). About 73% of the non-hylid road kills were on the 400 m of road beyond the barrier wall-culvert system (abstract wording), which suggests a fence-end experiment for the model. Abstract and summary checked.

## How many birds die on roads

**Rabie, P. A., Welsch, M. M., Stone, N. and Derby, C. (2024).** Novel approach to estimating avian mortality from vehicle-bird collisions on U.S. roads. *Journal of Field Ornithology* 95(3): 1. doi:10.5751/JFO-00498-950301. [Open access](https://journal.afonet.org/vol95/iss3/art1/)
About 96 million birds a year killed by vehicles in the continental USA (95% CI 59-200 million), estimated from collisions reported by drivers. Collision rates were highest on rural two-lane roads. Abstract checked.

**U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.** Threats to birds: collisions, road vehicles. [fws.gov](https://www.fws.gov/story/threats-birds-collisions-road-vehicles)
Cites an estimate of 89-340 million birds a year. The underlying paper (attributed elsewhere to Loss, Will and Marra) and its year are still to confirm; cite the paper, not this page, once found.

## Ducklings and their walk to water

**Talent, L. G., Jarvis, R. L. and Krapu, G. L. (1983).** Survival of mallard broods in south-central North Dakota. *The Condor* 85(1): 74-78. doi:10.2307/1367893. [SORA PDF](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v085n01/p0074-p0078.pdf)
25 radio-marked broods. Hens raised at least one duckling in 12 of the 25 broods. Whole broods were lost only in wetlands, never during overland travel, and mink were probably the main predator. Evidence that the overland walk itself, away from roads, costs little. Abstract checked.

**Dzus, E. H. and Clark, R. G. (1997).** Overland travel, food abundance, and wetland use by mallards: relationships with offspring survival. *The Wilson Bulletin* 109(3): 504-515. [SORA PDF](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v109n03/p0504-p0515.pdf)
52 radio-tracked broods near Yorkton, Saskatchewan. Duckling survival in the first two weeks was not related to the distance travelled to the first wetland, or to total overland distance. Abstract checked.

**Simpson, J. W., Yerkes, T., Nudds, T. D. and Smith, B. D. (2007).** Effects of habitat on mallard duckling survival in the Great Lakes region. *Journal of Wildlife Management* 71(6): 1885-1891. doi:10.2193/2006-204. [BioOne](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Wildlife-Management/volume-71/issue-6/2006-204/Effects-of-Habitat-on-Mallard-Duckling-Survival-in-the-Great/10.2193/2006-204.full)
116 broods, 2001-2003. Survival to 55 days rose with vegetated wetland area and fell with forest cover within 500 m; little support for an effect of overland distance. Abstract checked.

**Peterson, S. H., Ackerman, J. T., Hartman, C. A., Greenawalt, A. C., Casazza, M. L. and Herzog, M. P. (2024).** Duckling survival increased with availability of flooded wetland habitat and decreased with salinity concentrations in a brackish marsh. *Ornithological Applications* 126(3): duae017. doi:10.1093/ornithapp/duae017. Summarized in the USGS Western Ecological Research Center news release "Duckling survival is higher if nests are closer to wetland habitats flooded during the summer" (29 December 2025), [usgs.gov](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/werc/news/duckling-survival-higher-if-nests-are-closer-wetland-habitats-flooded-during)
556 radio-tagged mallard and gadwall ducklings from 284 broods in Suisun Marsh, California, 2016-2019. Per the release, broods hatched within about 140 m of flooded wetlands had at least a 75% chance of surviving the move to water; broods 970 m or more away had 50% or less. One of the few sources where distance to water does matter, and the one closest to the model's nest-choice trade-off. Paper abstract and release checked; the 140 m and 970 m figures are confirmed from the release only.

Together these four disagree about the cost of an overland walk without roads, which is why the model's `transitHazard` is still a placeholder (see PLAN.md, next steps).

## Stormwater ponds as ecological traps

**Sievers, M., Parris, K. M., Swearer, S. E. and Hale, R. (2018).** Stormwater wetlands can function as ecological traps for urban frogs. *Ecological Applications* 28(4): 1106-1115. doi:10.1002/eap.1714. [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/6ef85983-bc87-4733-9c10-4a0b365ccace)
Breeding frogs laid comparable numbers of eggs in wetlands with high and low contaminant levels, and tadpoles raised in mesocosms simulating more-polluted wetlands survived less. The authors describe it as the first empirical evidence of stormwater wetlands acting as ecological traps for frogs. Abstract checked; volume and pages confirmed from Crossref.

**Clevenot, L., Carré, C. and Pech, P. (2018).** A review of the factors that determine whether stormwater ponds are ecological traps and/or high-quality breeding sites for amphibians. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution* 6: 40. doi:10.3389/fevo.2018.00040. [PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fevo.2018.00040/pdf)
A review of 25 studies, mostly in Europe and North America. It establishes that highway and urban stormwater ponds are similar in structure and function, and lists the pond features that decide whether a pond is a trap. Relevant to the fenced retention pond in the geese story. Abstract checked.

## Ecological traps and evolutionary rescue

**Schlaepfer, M. A., Runge, M. C. and Sherman, P. W. (2002).** Ecological and evolutionary traps. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 17(10): 474-480. doi:10.1016/S0169-5347(02)02580-6. [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/5224180)
Sudden human changes to the environment can decouple formerly reliable cues from the outcomes they used to predict, trapping organisms that respond to them; a trapped population can go extinct if it falls below a critical size before adapting. The framing for the nest-choice mismatch and E10. Abstract checked (one source gives pages 478-480; USGS gives 474-480).

**Gomulkiewicz, R. and Holt, R. D. (1995).** When does evolution by natural selection prevent extinction? *Evolution* 49(1): 201-207. doi:10.1111/j.1558-5646.1995.tb05971.x. [Gomulkiewicz lab publications](https://labs.wsu.edu/gomulki/publications/)
Credited with coining "evolutionary rescue": a population declining after an abrupt environmental change persists only if adaptation outpaces the decline. Citation confirmed from Crossref. The paper models a population after an abrupt shift in its optimal phenotype and compares the time to fall below a critical size with the time to adapt; this is from secondary descriptions, since the abstract and full text have not been read.

## Street-network form

**Southworth, M. and Ben-Joseph, E. (1995).** Street standards and the shaping of suburbia. *Journal of the American Planning Association* 61(1): 65-81. doi:10.1080/01944369508975620.
How residential street standards evolved (in five historical periods, from 1800 on) and shaped the curvilinear, cul-de-sac suburban street network, the basis for the model's `suburban` layout. Citation confirmed from Crossref; abstract checked via the TRID record.

**Boeing, G. (2021).** Off the grid... and back again? The recent evolution of American street network planning and design. *Journal of the American Planning Association* 87(1): 123-137. doi:10.1080/01944363.2020.1819382. [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.04771), [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2d37f518/qt2d37f518.pdf)
A tract-level analysis of US street networks: grid-like design declined from 1940 through the 1990s and recovered somewhat after 2000. Grounds the model's comparison of grid and suburban layouts in what has actually been built. Abstract checked.
